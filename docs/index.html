How to read it. This is the table you cannot get in one click. A percentage move means nothing until you know the fund's normal daily range, so each move is also shown as a z-score: how many standard deviations it sits from that fund's average day over the past year. Roughly speaking, most days land inside one standard deviation and anything past two is worth a second look. But treat those thresholds loosely - market returns have fat tails, meaning extreme days occur far more often than the normal distribution predicts. That gap between the bell curve and reality is where a great deal of risk-management practice lives, and where a great deal of money has been lost by people who assumed otherwise. Volatility is the annualised standard deviation of recent daily moves; its percentile says where that sits against the fund's own past year, which is what distinguishes a calm market from a stressed one. Drawdown measures the fall from the highest close of the past year - closing prices only, so a spike low during the trading day is not captured.

What this is testing. This measures whether the yield story above is currently true, using a rolling window of recent trading days. Each day it asks: when the 10-year yield rose, did the fund tend to fall? A negative reading means yes - the textbook relationship is holding. Near zero means yields simply are not what is driving equities at the moment. Positive means they are rising and falling together, which typically happens when both are responding to stronger growth expectations rather than to inflation or policy fears. Three warnings. Correlation is not causation: both series can be responding to a third thing entirely. A rolling window is noisy, and one dramatic day entering or leaving the window can swing the line visibly. And a relationship that holds for months can break in a week - which is precisely why it is worth watching a measurement rather than memorising a rule.

Why this matters. The 10-year Treasury yield is the market's baseline for pricing almost everything else. A share is worth the cash it will produce in future, discounted back to today; the yield is a large part of that discount rate. When it rises, future cash is worth less now - and the further out the cash sits, the more it is marked down. Growth and technology names earn much of their value from distant profits, so they carry longer 'duration' and tend to react more sharply than an index of established, cash-generating firms. Why both lines start at 100. Both lines start at 100 so they can share one axis. This matters: plotting two prices at different levels on two different y-axes lets you align the scales to manufacture almost any correlation you like, which is why dual-axis charts are the single most misleading chart in finance. CPI - Consumer Price Index (July data)

The headline inflation number the public and the press react to. It is not the Fed's preferred gauge, but it is released earlier than PCE and reliably moves rates and equities on the day.

Higher than expected: Inflation looks stickier than hoped. Markets push out expected rate cuts or price a greater chance of further tightening; Treasury yields typically rise, and long-duration assets such as technology usually fall hardest. Risk appetite normally weakens.

Lower than expected: Disinflation looks on track. Expected policy easing is pulled forward, yields typically fall, and rate-sensitive and growth assets usually lead the rally.

2026-08-13in 5 daysdate unconfirmed - verify
PPI - Producer Price Index (July data)

Prices at the factory gate rather than the till. It matters for two reasons: producer costs feed into consumer prices with a lag, and several PPI components feed directly into the PCE calculation, so forecasters revise their PCE estimates within minutes of its release.

Higher than expected: Cost pressure is building upstream. Reinforces a hot CPI and raises expected PCE; usually pushes yields up. On its own it moves markets less than CPI.

Lower than expected: Cost pressure is easing before it reaches consumers. Supports the disinflation case and tends to pull expected PCE down with it.

FOMC minutes (July 28-29 meeting)

The statement tells you what the Committee decided; the minutes tell you how divided they were getting there. Markets read them for the spread of opinion and for the conditions officials named as triggers for changing course.

Higher than expected: Read here as more hawkish than the statement implied - more members worried about inflation, or a higher bar for easing. Yields typically rise and equities soften.

Lower than expected: Read here as more dovish - more concern about employment or growth, or a lower bar for easing. Supportive for both bonds and equities.

PCE price index (July data)

The Fed's preferred inflation measure, and specifically core PCE, which strips out food and energy. This is the number the Committee's own target is written in terms of, even though CPI generates the louder headlines. By release day much of it is already forecastable from CPI and PPI, so the surprise is usually smaller.

Higher than expected: Inflation is running above the Fed's own yardstick. The most direct challenge to any easing case; yields typically rise.

Lower than expected: Progress on the measure the Fed actually targets. Strengthens the case for easing more than a soft CPI does.

Employment Situation - the jobs report (August data)

Payrolls, the unemployment rate and average hourly earnings in one release. Usually the single largest scheduled mover of the month, because it speaks to both halves of the Fed's mandate at once.

Higher than expected: Ambiguous by design, and worth understanding properly. A hot jobs number is good for company earnings but reduces the pressure on the Fed to ease - so in a market focused on rates, good news for the economy can be bad news for stocks. Which effect wins depends on what the market is currently most afraid of.

Lower than expected: Also two-sided. A mild miss raises hopes of easing and equities often rally. A severe miss shifts the fear from inflation to recession, and then bonds rally while equities fall with them. Watch which one is happening.

FOMC interest rate decision + economic projections

Falls just outside the next 30 days. The rate decision itself is usually priced in well beforehand, so the market reaction comes from three other things: the wording changes in the statement, the Summary of Economic Projections (the 'dot plot', published only at the March, June, September and December meetings), and the Chair's press conference. September is a projection meeting, so officials publish where they each expect rates to sit in future years.

Higher than expected: A more hawkish outcome than priced - a higher dot plot, firmer statement language, or a Chair pushing back on easing expectations. Yields up, equities down, and typically a stronger dollar.

Lower than expected: A more dovish outcome - a lower dot plot or softer language. Yields down and risk assets up, with the longest-duration assets moving most.

Why a calendar belongs here. Scheduled releases are the days markets have already agreed to care about, which is what makes them tradeable events rather than news. What moves prices is never the number itself but the gap between the number and what was expected - a strong reading that everyone already anticipated moves nothing at all. That is why each entry below reads in both directions rather than declaring one outcome good.
